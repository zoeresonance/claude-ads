## Data & Methodology

We use a multifaceted approach to understand your community's nuances and gain a deep insight into its residents' demographics, psychographics, and behavior. This knowledge forms the foundation of your "Key Persona" and "One."

Our primary sources of data are industry leaders, ERSI and Kantar. Kantar is a vital part of WPP, the world's leading advertising agency. These organizations gather extensive consumer data through an exhaustive collection process that includes credit card transactions, online behaviors, retail purchases, social media engagements, media consumption patterns, surveys, mobile phone records, and the US Census. Their data scientists construct precise population profiles and accurately predict future behaviors.

We also use supplementary resources such as Gallup, Pew, PPRI, and the Association of Religion Data Archives (ARDA) to create a comprehensive composite of your community.

Our proprietary system, which is a culmination of tested best practices, decades of hands-on experience, and in-house data analysis, transforms this foundational information into your Key Persona and One.

**Demographics** are statistical data related to the population and specific groups within a given area.

**Psychographics** is the study and classification of people based on their attitudes, aspirations, and other psychological factors.

We analyze the population from two perspectives. The first considers the entire population; the second focuses on the people most likely to expand the church. We begin by examining demographics and psychographics of the population within a ten- and twenty-minute drive of the church.

## Section 5: The One & Key Persona — Framework

We have two distinct audiences. The first audience, known as your "Key Persona," is broad and encompasses both genders, with a wider age and income range. The second audience is your "One," which is a highly defined audience within your Key Persona. Our main goal is to reach your Key Persona through your One.

Your One is the most crucial person in your Key Persona, and you must have a clear understanding of this person to make decisions based on whether the ministry, event, or design is right for them. While the goal is to reach everyone, focusing on this specific individual will help you create corporate ministries, communications, branding, and marketing that will appeal to your One and, in turn, drive growth in other areas.

### Immediate Neighborhood (0–10 Min · 209,245 people)

Three LifeMode groups dominate this radius, accounting for roughly four out of every five residents. The immediate radius is defined by a collision of two very different household economies living side by side — and that collision is the single most important fact about this ring.

| LifeMode Group | % of Population |
|---|---|
| Urban Harmony | 27.0% |
| Suburban Shine | 25.4% |
| Metro Vibes | 25.3% |

**Urban Harmony (27.0%)**
The largest single group in the immediate radius, built on multigenerational households — married couples with or without children, often sharing a roof with extended family, with a significant share born outside the United States. Housing costs consume a substantial share of income; residents lean on public transportation for commuting, errands, and dining out. The most representative sub-segments in a dense Chicago-area setting are F3 – Downtown Melting Pot and F4 – City Strivers: households earning $74,000–$81,000, roughly a third to half holding some college or a bachelor's degree, working in a mix of professional and service roles — health care, administration, food service, transportation. Rent burden is real (45%+ of these households spend more than 35% of income on housing), but so is upward mobility — nearly a third have some college experience or better.

**Suburban Shine (25.4%)**
Nearly matching Urban Harmony in size but describing an entirely different life stage: householders 45 and older, established homeowners, preparing for or already easing into retirement. This is the Midwest-suburb archetype — single-family homes built in the second half of the last century, above-average home values, high labor-force participation often with two incomes, and steady employment in skilled office, administrative, and trade roles. Financially, this group is comfortable rather than wealthy — middle-tier household incomes paired with meaningfully higher net worth, largely built through decades of home equity. They are locally rooted, brand-loyal, and trust word-of-mouth.

**Metro Vibes (25.3%)**
The connective tissue between Urban Harmony and Suburban Shine. This group mixes families and individuals in their 30s with a notable recent-immigrant presence, working in health care, retail, and food service. Incomes are modest ($47,000–$70,000), homeownership is a stretch but achievable for some sub-segments, and roughly a quarter to a third of the population holds a bachelor's degree. Financially, these households are careful — deal-seeking, generic-brand-buying, credit-card-balance-carrying — but not without ambition; several sub-segments show above-average labor force participation and rising education levels among the under-40 cohort.

**Two Structural Takeaways**

This is not a market with one dominant story — it's a market with two closely matched majorities living inside the same 20-minute radius. Roughly 56% of the population sits in the working- to middle-class, immigrant-anchored family groups. At the same time, roughly 45% sits in the professionalized, higher-education, higher-income groups.

These two majorities are not strangers to each other — they are frequently the same extended family at different life stages. The data pattern (large working-class immigrant groups in the inner ring, large professionalized groups dominating the outer ring, both still substantial across the full radius) is the signature of a community where the second generation moves up and out a few miles, but stays close, stays connected, and stays part of the same extended household network.

**Three Generations of One Market**

Combined, the three segments do most of the work — and their profiles read like three chapters of the same family story.

- **Chapter one is Diverse Horizons:** the market's single largest segment at every radius, immigrant-anchored, with large families, modest incomes, and foreign-language fluency — the grandparents' and parents' generation.
- **Chapter two is Family Extensions and Dreambelt:** the working- and middle-class multigenerational household layer that bridges renting and homeownership.
- **Chapter three is Trendsetters:** the professionalized, bachelor's-degree-holding, tech- and healthcare-employed adult children of that same immigrant story, now running their own households a few minutes farther out.

Read together, these three segments are not three markets — they are three generations of one market, and Belmont AG sits at the geographic center of all three.

## Section 7: Key Persona — The Bridge Generation

### Overview

Primarily located 10–20 minutes from Belmont, these households represent the emerging edge of its service area — not the immediate neighborhood around the building. They are largely dual-income professionals in their 30s, living in newer rental communities and starter homes while working hybrid or remote roles in tech, healthcare, and education. Many are first-generation college graduates and recent immigrants, maintaining strong financial and relational ties to the established immigrant communities where they grew up.

> **43,873** adults within a 20-minute drive.

**Education and Income:** These households are climbing, not arrived — and that distinction matters more than the number itself. Just over half hold a bachelor's degree (53.4% in the core segment), working in technology, health care, and education, often the first in their family to finish college. Household incomes cluster around $75,000–$110,000 (segment median: $91,444), meaningfully lower than an established professional-class persona, but on a clear upward trajectory. Most are dual-income by necessity as much as ambition — one paycheck alone doesn't yet cover the gap between where they came from and where they're headed.

**Homeownership:** Most are renters or recent first-time buyers, not long-tenured homeowners. They live in newer apartment complexes or starter single-family homes in the outer edge of the service area — the 10–20 minute ring, not the immediate neighborhood — often within a short drive of the working-class, immigrant-anchored household they grew up in. Tenure in their current home tends to run 2–5 years, not 5–15. Their neighborhood is a step up from where they started, and they know it.

### Motivations

The motivating factors driving these families can be categorized into five core principles.

1. **Proving the Climb Was Worth It.** Every credential, promotion, and lease upgrade is validation — not just for themselves, but for the parents and grandparents who sacrificed to make it possible. Success is never fully private; it's owed to someone.
2. **Family Bridging.** Belmont's Bridge-Generation Builders carry active, ongoing responsibility to an older generation — translation, transportation, financial support, caregiving — even while building their own household. This isn't occasional; it's a standing line item in their week.
3. **Financial Foundation-Building.** Unlike an already-secure household, these families are still constructing their safety net. Stability isn't assumed — it's the active goal. They save deliberately, monitor debt closely, and measure progress by specific milestones (a degree completed, a lease upgraded, a first home purchased) rather than by a comfortable plateau.
4. **Bicultural Identity Integration.** They are not choosing between their heritage and their mobility — they're actively building a life that embraces both. Losing the language, the food, and the closeness of family is not an acceptable cost of moving up.
5. **Belonging Without Erasure.** They want to belong to their new community — the schools, the workplace, the social circle — without being asked to leave their culture at the door. They can tell the difference between a place that welcomes them and one that merely tolerates them.

### Lifestyle

**Activities:** Time is scarce and triple-booked — their own kids' school and activities, their job, and standing obligations to their parents' household. Family gatherings, not solo hobbies, are where social life actually happens. When they do carve out personal time, it's brief and intentional rather than leisurely.

**Technology:** Mobile-first and fluent, shaped by the same digitally-native behavior Esri documents for this segment — comfortable with digital payments, remote work tools, and telehealth. (Grounded in segment data.) Layered on top, and specific to a bicultural household — this part is informed inference, not a direct data point — is bilingual media consumption and platforms like WhatsApp for staying connected with family, including relatives abroad. At church, they expect the same low-friction digital experience they get everywhere else: simple sign-ups, clear information, no friction, and — critically — content available in both languages, not English with a translated afterthought.

### Faith & Identity

**Religion:** Most were raised Catholic — the dominant tradition in their family's country of origin, and still the largest single tradition in Belmont's service area at 29% self-identified, well above the 19% national rate. But the practice-to-identity gap is wide and measurable: 83% attended church regularly growing up, while only 42% are religiously active today by Gallup's index. Certainty of belief has softened alongside that drop-off — "absolutely certain" belief in God fell from 56% to 48% in a decade, and belief in heaven from 69% to 60%. This is not collapse; 84% still believe in a soul or spirit, and 79% say religion was very or somewhat important growing up. It's drift, not departure — and Chicago's religiously unaffiliated share has grown from 22% to 32% in that same decade, with this population a real part of that shift. They're not hostile to church — they're unconvinced any specific one is worth the time it used to get automatically.

Evangelical identity, notably, is the minority position here: just 10% self-identify as Evangelical locally, against 23% nationally, meaning a Bridge-Generation Builder raised Catholic is walking into genuinely unfamiliar territory at an AG church, not a neighboring denomination. They'll engage with a church that's relational, bilingual without being clumsy about it, and treats their family's Catholic heritage as the starting point rather than as something to be converted away from — rather than one that feels culturally foreign or reduces their background to an outreach target.

**Self-Identity:** They see themselves as competent, responsible parents and good neighbors — aspirational yet grounded. They prize authenticity over flash, excellence over spectacle, and community over cliques. Above all, they want to be the kind of family that shows up for each other and for their community.

### Future Goals

Build lasting financial security for their own household while continuing to support their parents'. Raise children who are fully fluent in both the language and culture of their heritage and the world they're moving into — not one at the expense of the other. Find a faith community, workplace, and neighborhood that treats their bicultural reality as a strength to be built around, not a complication to be managed. Long-term, they picture their children never having to choose between honoring where the family came from and fully belonging where they're going.

### Communication

**They are moved by:**
- **Family** — messaging that is family-inclusive and programming that visibly strengthens their marriage, their kids, and their relationship with their parents will perform well. This is the throughline, not one value among several.
- Real stories from people who've built something — a career, a family, a stable life — told with warmth and specificity, not polish
- Messages that speak directly to their actual week: career growth, raising young kids, and the financial and time pressure of doing both well
- Churches that feel relationally consistent and emotionally safe, where they can show up as they are
- A genuine nod to their family's heritage — a detail, a story, a shared reference — that feels like recognition, not a pitch

**They will tune out:**
- Religious jargon, especially language that implicitly frames their Catholic upbringing as something to be corrected rather than built on
- Overproduced "show" experiences that feel like a performance for a crowd rather than a relationship with a person
- Messaging that treats their bilingual or immigrant background as a diversity talking point rather than simply who they are
- Anything that reads as asking them to choose between honoring where they came from and belonging where they're going

What brings them back isn't tradition or polish — it's relevance to the specific, dual-pressure life they're actually living. A reintroduction to faith that meets them mid-climb, not one that assumes they're either fully assimilated or freshly arrived.

If you earn their trust, they won't just show up — they'll bring their parents to one service and their coworkers to another, because they're already the connector between both worlds. That's not a bonus outcome; it's the whole strategic bet on this persona.

### Channels & Tech

- **Text and mobile app notifications** are the most reliable way to reach this person directly — they're checking their phone constantly, managing their own schedule and their parents' in the same day.
- **Email:** As professionals, they are constantly checking email. Newsletters and well-designed, timed e-blasts are very effective.
- **Social media — Instagram primarily** — is where they'll discover and follow a church, more than where they expect direct communication.
- **Website** needs to be clean and fast on mobile, since that's how they'll actually access it, not a desktop-first design.
- **WhatsApp** is the one channel that crosses generations in this household — it's how they stay in touch with their parents and relatives day to day, including family abroad. It's less a "communication preference" for church outreach specifically and more a signal: if a church wants to reach the parents' generation through this person, a WhatsApp group or broadcast list will land far better than an email newsletter ever would, because that's the channel this family already lives in together.

### Brand Preferences

This audience is loyal to brands that make a full life simpler — not brands that merely signal status. They connect most with brands like Apple, Amazon, Nike, Netflix, and Spotify because those brands pair convenience with a clear, authentic identity.

They value authentic storytelling, consistency, and reliable services, as reflected in their adoption of subscriptions, meal kits, and flexible payment options. For Belmont, the opportunity is not a single impressive campaign, but a consistent, authentic experience that earns trust over time — and turns initial interest into lasting commitment.

### Brand Engagement Strategies

To reach this audience, lead with competence, family, and practical relevance. Messaging should speak to their priorities: building a stable career, raising their children well, and staying connected to their parents. Heritage matters, but it should feel like an authentic part of their story — not the centerpiece.

Family is their strongest motivator. Campaigns that visibly strengthen marriages, support parents, invest in kids, or deepen family connection will resonate most. Use clear, practical content delivered through mobile-first channels: text for direct communication, Instagram for discovery, and WhatsApp to reach the wider family — including Spanish-speaking parents and relatives.

### Messaging — Most Likely to Respond To

#### Parenting & Family

**Family as the Center, Not Just the Kids:** Lead with family, not just parenting — this is the throughline for Camila more than any other single theme. Show how your church strengthens the whole family unit, including her relationship with her parents, not just her role as a mom.

**Practical Support:** Reassure her that her daughter is genuinely safe and well cared for, so church becomes one less thing she has to manage rather than one more thing to worry about.

**A Place Everyone Actually Wants to Be:** Show that church is somewhere her daughter looks forward to, and — just as importantly — somewhere her parents would feel comfortable and welcome too.

**Faith as a Gift, Not an Obligation:** Frame nurturing her daughter's faith as giving her something valuable, not as a duty Camila is behind on.

**Time, Not FOMO:** Camila isn't driven by fear of her daughter missing out — she's driven by not having enough time. Messaging should emphasize how easy and low-effort it is to be part of, not how much is happening that she might miss.

**A Family That Works Together:** Show how your church supports the whole family system she's managing — her marriage, her daughter, and her ongoing role supporting her parents — as one connected picture, not separate problems to solve.

#### Personal

**Belonging Without Explanation:** Emphasize a community where she doesn't have to explain or justify who she is — she's simply welcomed as a capable adult building a good life.

**Being Seen as Enough:** Reassure her that she doesn't need to have it all figured out or be doing more than she already is to belong.

**Low-Pressure Welcome:** Highlight that showing up doesn't require commitment or explanation upfront — she can enter at her own pace.

#### Anxiety

**Relief, Not Another Obligation:** Position church as somewhere the weight gets lighter, not somewhere else that needs her time and energy.

**She's Not the Only One Holding It Together:** Let her know that being stretched between her own family and her parents' needs is common and understood here, not something she has to hide or manage alone.

#### Community

**A Few Real Relationships, Not a Big Network:** Emphasize genuine, low-maintenance friendships rather than a large social scene she has to keep up with.

**Room for Her Whole Family:** Highlight that this community has space for her parents and relatives too — not just her, individually.

#### Religion/Spirituality

**Come As You Are, Practice or Not:** Make clear that a lapsed relationship with church isn't a barrier — many people here are rebuilding that relationship too, not starting from a place of already having it figured out.

**Rooted, Not Replaced:** Frame this church as building on her Catholic upbringing, not asking her to abandon it — the destination may be different, but the foundation is respected, not dismissed.

**Fits Her Actual Life:** Emphasize that faith here is designed to work with a full, busy schedule — not something that requires her to already have more margin than she has.

**Family-Wide Welcome:** Reinforce that people of any faith background — and any level of current practice — are welcome, including relatives who may be more traditionally Catholic or more Spanish-speaking.

**Meets Her Where She Is:** Convey that this isn't about correcting her past experience, but about offering something that fits the life she's actually living right now — busy, dual-obligation, still building.

### Messaging — Least Likely to Respond To

When communicating with Camila, avoid language that implies she's failing to do enough — for her daughter, her parents, or her faith. She's already carrying real weight across two households, and messaging that adds guilt on top of that will push her away rather than draw her in. Avoid questioning her parenting or her choices around how she balances her obligations; this is sensitive territory given how stretched she already feels.

Avoid centering her heritage or bicultural background as the main hook — a respectful nod is welcome, but leading with it, or treating her as a diversity segment to be specifically reached, will read as othering rather than seen. Avoid messaging that assumes she's fluent in religious language or already comfortable in a church setting — she's not starting from zero, but she's also not currently active, so avoid assuming shared insider knowledge. Finally, avoid asking for more commitment or responsibility upfront; she has very little margin left to give, and messaging that reads as "one more thing" will lose her quickly.
